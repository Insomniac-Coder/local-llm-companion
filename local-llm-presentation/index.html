01 What is an LLM

A machine that has read more than any person ever could

A large language model (LLM) is a program trained on an enormous amount of text: books, websites, manuals, code. From all that reading it learned one skill extremely well. Given some text, it predicts what comes next. Ask it a question, and it predicts, word by word, what a good answer looks like.

Large

Billions of adjustable numbers, called parameters, hold everything it learned. An 8-billion-parameter model runs on a laptop; a 70-billion one on a single server.

Language

It reads and writes text: English, Hindi, code, tables. Newer models also read images and audio.

Model

A mathematical approximation of patterns in language. Not a database of facts, which is why it can sound right and still be wrong.

Engineer's note

Memory for the weights is roughly parameters × bits per parameter ÷ 8. At 16-bit an 8B model needs 16 GB; at 4-bit quantization about 4.5–5 GB, and a 70B model about 40 GB. Add memory for the conversation itself (the KV cache), which grows with the context length. Mixture-of-experts models such as DeepSeek-V3 (671B total, 37B active per token) must still hold all weights in memory, but only compute with the active ones, so they run at the speed of a much smaller model.

02 How it learns

It learns by guessing the next word, trillions of times

Nobody programs the knowledge in. The model starts as random numbers and improves by guessing, checking against real text, and adjusting itself after every mistake.

  1. Stage 1

    Pre-training: read everything

    It reads a huge slice of the internet, books and code. For every sentence it guesses the next word, compares with the real one, and nudges its numbers so the right word becomes more likely.

    15 trillion+ tokens read by Meta's Llama 3 models. The largest used about 31 million GPU-hours of computing.

  2. Stage 2

    Instruction tuning: learn to be an assistant

    It then studies many thousands of example conversations, written or checked by people: a question, followed by a good answer. It learns to follow instructions.

    Result: it answers questions instead of just continuing text.

  3. Stage 3

    Preference tuning: learn what people prefer

    People, and increasingly other models, compare pairs of answers and pick the better one. The model is nudged towards helpful, honest, safe answers.

    Result: the polished assistant you know from ChatGPT or Claude.

Engineer's note

Pre-training minimises cross-entropy loss on next-token prediction with gradient descent over mini-batches of millions of tokens across thousands of GPUs. Stage 2 is supervised fine-tuning (SFT); stage 3 is RLHF or a direct method such as DPO. Reasoning models add reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (unit tests, exact answers). Meta reports about 30.8 million H100 GPU-hours for Llama 3.1 405B.

Add it, and repeat

A 500-word answer means about 650 trips through the whole model. That is why answers stream in word by word.

Engineer's note

This is the transformer. Inference has two phases: prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel (compute-bound, thousands of tokens per second on a GPU) and decode generates one token per forward pass (memory-bandwidth-bound: every step reads all active weights). Keys and values of earlier tokens are kept in a KV cache so they are not recomputed. A useful ceiling for decode speed is memory bandwidth ÷ bytes of active weights, which is why quantization and mixture-of-experts models speed up generation.

The part most people miss

After training, the model is frozen.

It does not learn from your conversations.

Its knowledge stops at a cut-off date.

It has never seen our contracts, code or policies.

The fix: hand it the right documents at the moment a question is asked. That is called RAG, and it is the heart of this proposal.

04 Rent or own

Rent a seat, or own the engine

ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot run in the vendor's data centre. Our own AI runs on servers we control, on-site or in our own cloud account, and the model file is ours to keep.

A fair comparison: the best subscription models still lead on the hardest problems. For everyday work (drafting, summarising, answering from our documents, help with code) open-weight models are more than good enough, and only they keep sensitive data in the building.

Engineer's note

“Our own” does not have to mean a server room. The same software runs on GPU instances in our own cloud account, in a region we choose, with no third-party model provider in the path. Business and enterprise subscription plans usually promise not to train on customer data and offer retention controls, but prompts and documents are still processed and stored on the vendor's infrastructure under their policies, and subject to the legal processes of their jurisdiction.

06 Our harness

The model is the engine. The harness is the rest of the car.

A harness is the software around the model. It finds the right documents, checks who is asking, decides which tools the model may use, applies our rules and keeps a record. ChatGPT and Claude are harnesses around their vendors' models. Build our own, and every one of those decisions is ours.

  • Which model runs, and when to upgrade it, without rebuilding anything around it.
  • What it can read: which systems are connected, and who may see what.
  • What it can do: which tools it may use, and which actions need a person's approval.
  • What it remembers: what is logged, for how long, and who can review it.
  • How it is measured: our own test questions, run before every change.
  • What it costs: our hardware, our budget, no per-question billing.
Engineer's note

Target architecture: an inference server (vLLM for many users, llama.cpp for small machines) behind an OpenAI-compatible API; an ingestion service with connectors, chunking, embeddings, hybrid search and a reranker; a policy layer that enforces single sign-on identity and document permissions at retrieval time; tools exposed through MCP servers with an allow-list and human approval for write actions; defences against prompt injection in retrieved content; an evaluation set of real questions run on every model or prompt change; and tracing of every request. Because the harness talks to models through one API, swapping the model is a configuration change.

07 Already built

We have already built a harness like this

The PC Companion is a working prototype, built in-house. It runs open-weight models entirely on our own hardware, down to a single laptop: chat, questions about code, and an agent that reads and writes project files, runs commands and checks its own work. Permission modes decide what the agent may do without asking, memory is managed automatically on long tasks, and every step is logged on disk.

A company deployment would run the same ideas on a shared server for many people at once.

Engineer's note

A RAG pipeline: connectors pull documents from their sources; documents are split into passages and embedded; passages go into a vector index alongside keyword search (hybrid search usually beats either alone); a reranker orders the candidates; the top passages go into the prompt with instructions to cite them. Access-control lists are applied at retrieval time, so people only get answers from documents they may open. Fine-tuning today is mostly LoRA or QLoRA: a small adapter trained on one GPU in hours, useful for format, tone and narrow classification, poor at adding facts reliably.

09 The plan

Start small, measure honestly, then grow

Not a company-wide rollout. A pilot that answers one question with evidence: does our own AI, working from our own documents, save real time for real people?

  1. Weeks 1–2

    Choose

    Pick one department and its documents. Agree what success looks like. Legal reviews the licence of the model we select.

  2. Weeks 3–8

    Build the pilot

    One GPU server, bought or rented. An open-weight model with RAG over that department's documents, inside our harness with access rights and an audit log. Twenty to thirty pilot users.

  3. Weeks 9–12

    Measure

    Accuracy on a set of real questions with known answers. Time saved, from the users themselves. Cost per user compared with subscriptions.

  4. Next quarter

    Decide and grow

    If the numbers hold: more departments and data sources, connected tools, and fine-tuning only where tests show a clear gain.
